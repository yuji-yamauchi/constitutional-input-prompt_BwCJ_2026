# 03_monetization — Who carries the cost

If this were to keep running, who carries the cost. It is not built in this
submission; the thinking is kept here.

This is written from inside. The author runs an employment-transition support
office under Japan's disability welfare system — the kind of place that is paid
public benefit money per attending user, and employs staff out of it. So this is
not an outside analysis. It is one operator working out whether to put this tool
into their own office.

The Japanese original is at [`../ja/`](../ja/).

---

## 1. First, the four that do not change

The revenue model may change. These four do not.

1. **Do not sell judgments.**
   Scores and suitability verdicts are not the product. What can be sold is
   *a place where the person writes* — never *an answer about the person*.

2. **Do not sell the person's record.**
   "It is fine if we obtain consent" is not the test. Where people differ in how
   freely they can refuse, consent is another word for price.

3. **Do not charge for taking it out.**
   When someone leaves, they leave with what they wrote. Put a price on the exit
   and the record becomes a hostage.

4. **When the contract ends, the person's original stays with the person.**
   If the office cancels, what the person wrote remains on their side. What
   disappears is the office's copy.

These four narrow the space of possible business models. Narrowing it is the
point. Decide how to make money first and apply the ethics afterwards, and the
ethics usually pass. Draw the lines first and the bad options never get drafted.

---

## 2. Why this sits in "03"

Cost is a **result, not a condition**, for this tool.

Argue in the order "social security spending is rising, therefore raise the
quality of support", and on the day the number moves the other way, the same
logic says "therefore cut the entitlement". An argument founded on cost flips
the moment the cost does.

So the order is fixed. The foundation is the fact that **decisions about a person
are being made from records that person did not write.** Cost arrives afterwards.

---

## 3. The five layers, seen from the operator's side

The system a welfare operator actually needs has five layers.

| | Layer | What it holds |
|---|---|---|
| ① | **The operating company** | Whoever supplies the system |
| ② | **The corporate layer** | Running the company itself |
| ③ | **The office dashboard** | Day-to-day operations, records, billing |
| ④ | **Staff management** | Attendance, scheduling, chat |
| ⑤ | **The user's screen** | What the person themselves uses |

**Layers ① through ④ are already solved by ordinary business software.**
Attendance, scheduling, office records — several companies already sell these,
and operators are **already paying for them**.

**Only ⑤ does not exist yet.**
Products with a "user screen" do exist, but what is on them is *what the office
shows the person*. It is not **a place where the person writes.**

This tool is that ⑤. And it **connects at ③ and ④** — only what the person
decides to hand over arrives at the office's receiving side.

---

## 4. The money comes from ② ③ ④

This is the part this document exists to say.

> **⑤ is not sold. What ② ③ ④ earn is what carries ⑤.**

- ② ③ ④ are **costs the operator is already paying** — corporate administration,
  office operations, staff attendance. They are not a new line item.
- ⑤ rides on top as **the layer that connects to them**. Nothing is billed to the person.
- Which means **there is no need to sell the person's record.**

A proposal that comes up often: give it to users free, in exchange for letting
the records be used for training. It looks like a deal in which nobody loses.
But **the moment a person's record is the consideration, the record becomes a
hostage to the price.** "Hand it over if you cannot pay" takes most from those
who have least.

If ② ③ ④ carry it, that trade does not have to be made. That is the reason for
designing it this way.

### Why the order matters

Price ⑤ first, and the only options left are "charge the person" or "charge for
the person's data". Both run into the four lines at the top.

Put ② ③ ④ first, and ⑤ can stay **a layer with no price on it.**

---

## 5. Why the person cannot be the payer

The shape of an office's revenue makes the reason plain. No figures here — the
unit prices have to be re-derived from the fee schedule before they go in.

- **Almost all revenue is public money**, calculated from the days each user attended.
- **It is per-day, not per-month.**
- **Add-ons stack on top** — staffing levels, how many people got jobs, how many stayed.
- **Users pay almost nothing**; caps mean many pay nothing at all.

From that, one structure follows.

> **The person is not a paying customer.**

In a market, the people who use a thing pay for it, so their preferences travel
back into its design through price. This sector has no such path. The payer is
the public purse, and what travels back into design is **what the fee schedule
rewards.** So "what gets paid for" tends to become "what counts as support".

Keeping ⑤ unpriced is also a way of holding **the person's own screen** outside
that pressure.

---

## 6. What is not known yet

- **What ② ③ ④ are worth, and whether that covers ⑤.** Not calculated. When the
  figures go in, they will come from the fee schedule and public statistics.
- **How it is carried in the books.** A software fee or a contracted service
  changes both the accounting and the relationship with the person. Undecided.
- **No office has run it yet.** What is written here is a set of design
  conditions, not a record of something operated.
- **The regulatory side is moving.** Ahead of the next fee revision, the fees and
  criteria for the new work-choice support service are on the agenda — the
  discussion is unfinished and neither prices nor requirements are set. If a fee
  gets attached to assessment, the structure in §5 lands directly on this tool.
  That is when the four lines at the top start to matter.
